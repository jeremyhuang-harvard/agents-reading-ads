# Backgrounded brand exposure: conceptual framing and discriminating research program

## Core framing

Use **backgrounded brand exposure** as the umbrella: a brand-linked stimulus is available in the environment but is not the audience's focal task and does not foreground an explicit sales pitch.[7][12] This is a property of an **exposure episode**, not a single media category. A stadium logo, jersey sponsor, celebrity's branded object, product in a stream, native article, and organic press mention can all be backgrounded, but they differ in who paid, who controls the message, whether commercial intent is disclosed, how the brand is integrated, and what psychological mechanism should follow.[12][16][20]

Avoid using **implicit advertising** as a synonym for all such cases. “Implicit” can refer to low attention, absent awareness, implicit memory, unrecognized persuasion, or an indirect claim; those are different constructs.[7][8][16] Code them separately.

The central theoretical proposal is that low-salience exposure can do at least three economically distinct things:

1. **Cue an existing memory stock:** make prior consumption experiences or an already-known category more retrievable now.[1]
2. **Alter memory stock:** create familiarity or strengthen brand–context, brand–person, and brand–affect associations that can be retrieved later.[4][6][13]
3. **Change beliefs or defensive processing:** convey information, credibility, social proof, or persuasive intent—with effects depending on whether the audience recognizes advertising.[16][17][21]

These channels can coexist, but they imply different heterogeneity, spillovers, mediators, and time profiles.[1][2][16]

## Taxonomy: formats, exposure conditions, mechanisms, and provenance

| Construct | What defines it | Not equivalent to | Predicted first-order outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Product/brand placement** | Deliberate inclusion of a branded object or mention inside entertainment, editorial, game, or creator content; often paid or otherwise exchanged. Placement prominence and plot integration vary.[10][12] | Mere exposure or incidental exposure, which are mechanisms/attention conditions; unpaid organic use. | Memory is usually the largest effect; attitudes and choice are smaller on average. Plot connection tends to strengthen effects.[10] |
| **Sponsorship** | A resource exchange linking a brand to a property—team, event, league, venue, cause, program—with rights to communicate the association. The brand is a supporter/partner, not necessarily a product used in the content. | Product placement; an ambassador personally recommending the product. | Sponsor identification plus transfer of property associations; articulation and perceived fit strengthen the memory link.[13][15] |
| **Brand ambassador / endorser** | A person is intentionally linked to a brand as spokesperson, representative, user, or recurring partner. An ambassador usually implies an ongoing relationship; an endorsement can be a single execution. | Sponsorship of an event; incidental exposure to a stranger using a brand. | Source credibility/attractiveness, parasocial influence, and transfer of cultural meaning from person to brand.[14] |
| **Incidental exposure** | The brand is encountered while attention is allocated to another task or object. It is defined by processing conditions, not by payment or marketer intent.[7] | Subliminal exposure; product placement; earned media. Any of those may or may not be incidental. | Increased accessibility, fluency, consideration-set entry, or choice despite weak explicit recall.[7][8][9] |
| **Mere exposure** | Repetition increases liking or reduces aversion in the absence of new propositional information or reinforcement.[4][5] | A media format or proof of unconscious persuasion. Recognition and affect can dissociate, but “not recalled” does not establish no awareness at exposure. | Stimulus-specific familiarity/liking, often nonlinear in repetitions; excessive exposure can wear out.[4][5] |
| **Associative/evaluative learning** | Repeated or diagnostic co-occurrence links the brand with a context, person, affect, trait, or outcome; evaluative conditioning is the special case in which pairing changes liking.[6] | Mere exposure, which requires repetition of the focal stimulus but no paired associate; Ads as Cues retrieval of pre-existing consumption memories. | Changed brand–associate strength, attribute inference, or affect, with transfer governed by contingency, fit, and prior knowledge.[6][13][15] |
| **Covert/native advertising** | Paid or controlled persuasion adopts the form and delivery conventions of non-advertising content, making commercial intent harder to recognize.[16][18] | All low-salience ads; product placement that is recognized; earned media. | Effects depend on ad recognition and persuasion knowledge. Disclosure can increase recognition while reducing credibility or evaluation, but effects are format- and source-dependent.[16][17][18][19] |
| **Earned media / publicity** | Editorial or interpersonal attention not purchased as media inventory and not fully controlled by the focal brand—press coverage, reviews, organic sharing, word of mouth.[20][21] | Native advertising, paid influencer content, or PR-generated coverage over which the firm retains message control. “Earned” describes provenance, not neutrality. | Greater source credibility but less message control; sales effects can differ between traditional and social earned media.[20][21] |

### Measurement implication

Every exposure should be located on separate axes:

- `commercial_provenance`: paid/exchanged, owned, earned, unknown;
- `message_control`: brand-controlled to independent;
- `commercial_disclosure` and `ad_recognition`: separately observed;
- `integration`: adjacent, embedded, used by character/person, plot- or task-integrated;
- `perceptual_prominence`: size, duration, contrast, audio mention, foregrounding;
- `pitch_directness`: logo-only to explicit offer/CTA;
- `attentional_status`: focal, peripheral, incidental, unknown;
- `carrier`: event, person, character, editorial voice, peer, interface, physical environment;
- `semantic cue vector`: category, occasion, affect, identity, action, reward, risk, social norm;
- `dose`: exposure count, duration, spacing, and recency.

GABRIEL can measure content-side axes, but **incidental processing, recognition, and exposure dose are audience-level variables** and cannot be inferred from the creative alone.[7][16]

## Connection to *Ads as Cues* and “memory stock”

*Ads as Cues* models advertising as a retrieval cue. Current demand depends on the frequency of a consumer's prior experiences, similarity between the current cue and those experiences, and interference from competing memories. The same Pepsi cue can therefore retrieve Pepsi for one consumer and Coke for a habitual Coke buyer; the paper predicts own effects, positive spillovers to similar rivals, and negative interference for dissimilar wants, amplified by the target consumer's history.[1]

Three “stocks” must not be collapsed:

1. **Consumption-memory stock** (`f_g` in *Ads as Cues*): accumulated experiences with brand/category `g`. Exposure activates this stock; it need not create it.[1]
2. **Learned-association stock:** accumulated links between a brand and occasions, people, events, affects, or actions. Placements, sponsorships, and ambassadors can add or strengthen these links.[6][13][14]
3. **Advertising goodwill/adstock:** a reduced-form state variable through which past advertising expenditures continue to affect demand and decay over time. Nerlove–Arrow formalizes advertising as investment in goodwill but does not specify whether persistence comes from familiarity, associations, beliefs, or retrieval.[2]

Becker–Murphy provides another benchmark: advertising is a complement or substitute in utility rather than a retrieval technology.[3] The most useful contribution of a backgrounded-ad program is therefore not to show that unobtrusive exposure “works,” but to distinguish **temporary retrieval**, **durable encoding**, **utility complementarity**, **belief change**, and **persuasion-recognition effects**.[1][2][17]

## Most discriminating hypotheses

### H1. Prior-experience reversal

Randomized low-salience exposure should have a larger immediate effect among consumers with a richer **target/category consumption history**, potentially flattening among the most habitual users.[1] Mere exposure accounts instead predict gains from repetition without requiring prior consumption history.[4][5] This `exposure × prior experience` contrast is the cleanest first discriminator.

### H2. Consumer-specific competitor spillovers

A brand cue should increase choice/search for a **similar rival** when that rival dominates the viewer's own memory stock.[1] Thus the same exposure should produce heterogeneous brand outcomes based on prior purchases, while expanding category demand.[1][22][23] Generic persuasion or own-brand familiarity predicts own-brand lift; a cue account predicts `cue similarity × target-brand history`, including rival lift.[1]

### H3. Attitude–choice dissociation

If exposure changes choice or consideration without improving brand evaluation, it is more consistent with accessibility/retrieval than with evaluative conditioning or meaning transfer.[6][7][8] Incidental-ad studies show that weakly attended exposure can affect consideration and fluency, and placement studies show that explicit memory and choice can dissociate.[7][8][11] Measure brand liking, free recall, recognition, response latency, consideration, and actual choice separately.

### H4. Valence and identity transfer

Hold brand visibility constant while changing the carrier's affect, traits, or identity. Associative learning predicts selective transfer of those attributes to the brand, especially under repeated pairing.[6] Endorsement and sponsorship research further predicts stronger transfer when the relationship is articulated or fitting.[13][14][15] Pure retrieval predicts increased accessibility of existing experiences but no necessary change in rated brand traits or valence.[1]

### H5. Disclosure/recognition invariance versus persuasion-knowledge response

Cross disclosure and actual ad recognition while holding content and exposure constant. If disclosure operates mainly through persuasion knowledge, it should change source credibility, skepticism, and evaluations once commercial intent is recognized.[16][17][18] Evidence from sponsored social content shows that source and disclosure conditions can moderate this response.[19] A memory-cue effect may survive disclosure and skepticism if the cue still retrieves the category or past consumption; an informational or credibility channel should be more disclosure-sensitive.[1][21]

### H6. Cue similarity versus brand identity

Create de-branded or rival-branded versions with the same consumption occasion, sport, reward imagery, or sensory pattern. *Ads as Cues* predicts that response follows psychological similarity to stored experiences, not only the displayed logo.[1] Mere exposure predicts repetition-based affect toward the exposed stimulus, while associative learning predicts effects for the paired brand or associate.[4][6] Utility-complement models do not naturally predict history-dependent rival spillovers.[3]

### H7. Encoding–retrieval decomposition

Use two stages. At `t1`, vary ambient exposure/repetition and whether consumption occurs while the brand cue is present. At `t2`, independently vary decision-context cues. A current cue with no earlier exposure tests **retrieval**; earlier exposure with no current cue tests **stock formation/persistence**; matched `t1–t2` contexts test learned brand–occasion links.[1][2][6] This design separates cueing from adstock rather than treating any lagged effect as “memory.”

### H8. Interference signature

Insert competing cues between exposure and choice. Similar category cues may produce positive category spillovers, but dense competing exposure should reduce retrieval of a focal brand; sufficiently dissimilar salient cues may redirect demand toward other wants.[1] Simple cumulative adstock predicts persistence from the accumulated stock unless saturation or wearout is added; retrieval competition predicts effects from the **composition** of the cue environment.[1][2]

### H9. Provenance-content equivalence test

Present matched content as paid native material, disclosed sponsorship, independent editorial coverage, or peer sharing. If content-driven cueing is primary, semantic similarity and consumer history should explain a common component across provenance conditions.[1] If earned-media credibility or covert persuasion is primary, labeling/source should materially alter beliefs and evaluations.[16][20][21]

### H10. Ambient maintenance, not immediate conversion

For familiar betting brands, repeated low-salience logos and sport-linked sponsorship may chiefly maintain category/brand retrievability between betting occasions.[1][13] Withdrawal should produce gradual erosion and stronger effects when a relevant match, odds, payday, or prior betting context later supplies a retrieval cue.[1][2] A short-run click or wager regression can miss this channel; preregister distributed lags and event-triggered reactivation.[1]

## Concrete empirical program for betting and prediction-market advertising

### 1. Observational measurement

Extend the GABRIEL codebook with the axes above, especially `brand_prominence`, `pitch_directness`, `plot_or_task_integration`, `carrier_type`, `commercial_disclosure`, `sport_or_event_cue`, `betting_action_cue`, `reward_affect`, and multimodal cue-similarity embeddings. Keep paid/owned/earned provenance separate from inferred psychological mechanism.

### 2. Controlled mechanism experiment

Recruit current bettors, lapsed bettors, nonbettors, and users of competing operators using verified prior-use measures. Randomize a factorial subset of:

- low versus high prominence;
- logo identity versus de-branded category cue;
- cue similarity to the participant's usual operator/product/sport;
- positive/neutral carrier affect;
- paid/disclosed versus native/unlabeled versus editorial provenance;
- one versus spaced repeated exposures;
- immediate versus delayed choice context.

Primary outcomes: unaided brand/category retrieval, free association, recognition, brand-trait ratings, consideration set, incentive-compatible operator/market choice, wager allocation, and delayed return. The preregistered signature for *Ads as Cues* is `exposure × target history × cue similarity`, including competitor/category spillovers and interference—not a main effect of exposure alone.

### 3. Field design

Exploit or randomize naturally backgrounded exposures—broadcast/stadium sponsorship visibility, creator overlays, odds widgets, jersey signage, or event integrations—and link them to search, app opening, deposits, or wagers. Estimate effects by prior operator/category use and multimodal similarity to each consumer's historical product mix. Measure all major rivals and total betting, because own-brand outcomes alone cannot distinguish business stealing from category cueing.

## Caveats

- **Low salience is not no attention.** Eye tracking, recognition, and task probes are needed before claiming unconscious influence.[7][8]
- **Format does not identify mechanism.** A placement can inform, cue, condition, signal popularity, or activate persuasion knowledge.[10][12][16]
- **Explicit-memory nulls are weak evidence.** Failed recall after the fact does not prove the stimulus was never consciously perceived.[5][8][11]
- **History is endogenous.** Heavy bettors both possess more betting memories and differ in taste, opportunity, targeting, and susceptibility; random exposure and within-person or experimentally induced histories are needed.
- **Similarity must be validated psychologically.** Visual/text embeddings are useful design variables but do not establish which features consumers retrieve.
- ***Ads as Cues* is currently an NBER working paper, not peer-reviewed.** Its frequency–similarity–interference predictions are unusually discriminating, but the empirical estimates remain quasi-experimental.[1]
- **Earned media is not necessarily independent.** PR activity may generate coverage, and influencer or affiliate compensation can be hidden; provenance should permit `unknown` rather than force a label.[19][20][21]

## Bottom line

The useful umbrella is not “subtle advertising” but **backgrounded brand exposure with separately measured provenance, prominence, integration, recognition, and cue content**. The decisive theoretical split is between exposure that **retrieves an existing consumption memory**, exposure that **builds a durable association/familiarity stock**, and exposure that **changes beliefs or activates persuasion defenses**. For *Ads as Cues*, the strongest evidence would be consumer-specific, similarity-structured spillovers: the same innocuous cue changes which brand or betting action comes to mind according to the viewer's prior memory stock, even when brand attitudes and explicit ad recall do not move.
