Who is part of whom? Directed associations between celebrities and brands

Jeremy Huang · exploratory, public data only · every number below is observed in the named source unless marked

Similarity says Tate McRae and Adidas are “close.” It cannot say whether thinking of Tate calls up Adidas more than thinking of Adidas calls up Tate. We measure each direction separately, as the share of one entity's representation that is made of the other.

Bottom line

  1. Associations are directional, and the bigger side is usually the smaller component. A brand is a visible part of its ambassador's coverage and navigation; the ambassador is a sliver of the brand's.
  2. A young brand can flip it. After the September 9 campaign, Sydney Sweeney is 90% of Novig's news coverage, while Novig is 31% of hers; on Wikipedia, about a quarter of readers leaving Novig's page go to Sweeney's.
  3. Interference shows up on the brand side. A brand with many ambassadors points to one or two of them. Nike's coverage names LeBron in 9% of articles and each of its other ambassadors in under half a percent. The others are part of Nike's story without Nike being part of theirs (the “fan effect” in memory research).
  4. This is a sketch. Light samples (a few thousand articles, four months of Wikipedia clicks); the pipeline is built to scale once we choose what to measure.

1. The idea: composition, not similarity

An entity's representation is what is written and searched about it. Sarkar (“Economic Representations”) builds a firm's vector as the average embedding of its news coverage. Averaging makes the vector decompose: the share of Tate's vector that comes from Tate coverage mentioning Adidas is a well-defined number, and it is different from the share of Adidas's vector that comes from Adidas coverage mentioning Tate. Psychologists call the two directions forward and backward associative strength; Ads as Cues needs exactly this object, because a cue retrieves goods according to how strongly the cue points to them, and the goods it retrieves compete.

Measures used here, all directed: share of A's news articles that name B (GDELT, Common Crawl News); share of clicks leaving A's Wikipedia article that go to B's; share of A's related Google searches naming B; and how far A's embedding moves toward B after an event.

3. Brands with many ambassadors

The frame has 43 celebrities, 36 brands and 53 sourced relationships, built around hub brands: Adidas (11 ambassadors), Calvin Klein, Nike and Dior (5 each), Chanel (4), Louis Vuitton and Puma (3).

Full-text news

3,815 English news articles whose headline or description names a frame entity, from a light sample of 59 Common Crawl News files. For each pair with at least 10 articles about each side, the chart compares how often articles about the celebrity name the brand with how often articles about the brand name the celebrity. In 14 of 20 pairs the brand is the larger share of the celebrity. The reversals are the superstars: Rihanna is in a fifth of the Puma articles, LeBron in a tenth of the Nike articles, and Sweeney in a third of the American Eagle articles. Rising or niche ambassadors (A'ja Wilson, Anya Taylor-Joy, Kristen Stewart, Angel Reese, Anthony Edwards) carry the brand without being part of it. Nike's coverage names its biggest star (LeBron, 9%) and almost none of its other ambassadors (≤0.4% each): with many ambassadors, the brand side points to one.

4. Vectors: who moved toward whom (Sweeney × Novig so far)

Each entity's vector is the average sentence embedding (BAAI bge-base) of its English news headlines in a window (GDELT). For each event I compare the 90 days before (days −90 to −8) with the 30 days after. Celebrity → brand is how much closer the celebrity's vector moved to the brand's pre-event position; brand → celebrity is the reverse. Both subtract the same movement toward three placebo targets (rival brands; comparable celebrities), so general drift cancels. Units are changes in cosine similarity; brackets are 90% bootstrap intervals over headlines.

Brands retrieve attributes (country, logo, product, founder), almost never people. Celebrities sometimes retrieve their brand: LeBron James → Nike in 57% of samples, Kendall Jenner → Calvin Klein 53%, Charlize Theron → Dior 30%, Rihanna → Puma 23%. In 2,370 samples, no brand cue ever produced one of its ambassadors. This is the purest form of the asymmetry: the link runs only from person to brand.

6. What is light now, and how it scales

  • Full-text news at scale. Common Crawl News streams ~400 files (1 GB each) per month; we read ~2 per month here. Nothing is stored except matched articles (a few MB). Scaling to ~10 files per month gives ~5× the articles. Decision: which months and how many files.
  • Monthly Wikipedia clickstream back to 2024. Complete, directed, free; 0.5 GB streamed per month, a few MB kept. Gives a monthly panel of who-points-to-whom for every entity.
  • Vectors for all 18 dated events. Wear events (Taylor Swift's New Balance 550s, Nike AF1s, Kristin Juszczyk jacket; Meghan's Outland jeans) vs ambassador deals. The binding constraint is GDELT's rate limit (about one request a minute today), so this runs overnight.
  • Human benchmark. Small World of Words free-association norms (registration required) to anchor the model-based measures.
CaveatsPairs were assembled by hand to span size ratios and hub brands, not sampled. Shares depend on how names are matched (ambiguous names such as Puma or Jennie require context words). Wikipedia only publishes transitions seen at least 10 times a month. No result here is causal.
