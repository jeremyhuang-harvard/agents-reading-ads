Implicit Ads · Demo findings

When a celebrity wears a product, where does the attention go?

Five cases, collected from public sources on November 9, 2026 · repo jeremyhuang-harvard/implicit-ads, draft PR #1

We built a cached pipeline that follows a celebrity appearance from the product worn, to who identified it and when, to search attention, to whether the product page ran out of stock, and compared the worn product with its same-brand siblings and with competitors.

Bottom line

  1. The response landed on the exact item. In the cleanest case, Kanye’s all-white Energy Boost ESM went out of stock on adidas.com within a week, while four other colorways of the same shoe stayed in stock.
  2. Search spiked on the celebrity–brand pairing, not on the product category. “Taylor Swift New Balance” and “Justin Bieber Crocs” rose more than 11 standard deviations; generic searches for the model or brand barely moved.
  3. We found no detectable spillover to competitors in search or in news coverage. Same-brand spillover, where it appears, went to the closest sibling, and is confounded.
  4. This is consistent with the low-interference idea (a distinctive, exactly identified cue retrieves the focal product rather than the category), but five hand-picked events cannot establish it. Treat it as a hypothesis for the 30-appearance sample.

What we tried to observe

Each case is traced through the same chain. Dark bars mark links where public data gave us something we retrieved ourselves; grey marks links that rest mainly on press or company claims.

AppearanceDate, time, event, item worn.
IdentificationWho named the exact product, and how fast.
AmplificationArticles, shopping links, affiliate tracking.
AttentionGoogle Trends, Wikipedia pageviews.

Market response is the weakest link: we can see when a product page ran out of stock, but never how many units sold.

How to read the evidence

Every claim carries one of two labels:

  • observed we retrieved it ourselves (an archived product page, a search series, an article timestamp).
  • reported a company or press claim we could not check (for example, New Balance telling Complex that units rose 22%).
The search charts use standard deviations (“z-scores”) For each search term we compute what is normal for that weekday from the four weeks before the appearance. The bar is the extra search over days 0–3, divided by how much that term usually wobbles day to day. 0 is business as usual; 2 (the dotted line) is unusual; 11 is far outside anything seen in the prior month. It is not a percentage, and it does not compare how many people searched for different terms. Hatched bars are sparse series (mostly zero days before the event) whose z-scores are fragile.

Case: Kanye × Adidas Energy Boost ESM (May 9–10, 2015)

Kanye, already an Adidas partner, performed at Wango Tango and sat courtside at a Bulls–Cavaliers game in a plain all-white running shoe, not his own Yeezy. This is our cleanest case because the archived product pages cover both the worn colorway and its siblings.

Identification
Complex named the “Energy Boost ESM in Running White” within hours, shortly before midnight Pacific on the night of Wango Tango. observed
Who covered it
All on-topic articles were sneaker-specialist outlets (Complex, Nice Kicks, WearTesters, Sneaker News). observed
Search
“Energy Boost” 3.8 SD above baseline over days 0–7. observed
Stock
In stock in all 11 captures from January to day +5; out of stock with Add to Bag disabled on days +6, +7, +9, +10. observed
Resale
eBay asks reached $1,000 but went unsold; highest completed sale $153, below the $160 retail price. reported

Takeaway: a clear stockout of the exact item, with no resale premium. Hype is not the same as excess demand at retail price.

Case: Taylor Swift × New Balance 550 (September 24, 2023)

No known relationship with New Balance (her footwear deal was with Keds, 2012–13). She left Arrowhead Stadium in a Chiefs windbreaker and white/red 550s. This case has the best reported sales numbers and the clearest view of shopping links.

Identification
Esquire published a “White & Red 550” shop link the next evening; The Hollywood Reporter named the style code BB550SE1. observed
Search
“Taylor Swift New Balance” went from about 1 to 100 by day +2. Generic “New Balance 550” rose only on the Monday and Tuesday (86 and 83 against ~60). observed
Shopping links
10 archived NB product URLs carry tracking: 8 affiliate (NY Post network, Penske Media, Skimbit, Sneaker News) and 2 New Balance email. observed
Stock
BB550SE1 had 19 of 20 sizes on day +2 and 18 of 20 on days +3 to +7. No capture exists from before the game, so we cannot see the reported sellout. observed
Sales
Week after: product-page traffic +30%, units +22%, revenue +25% (New Balance to Complex). StockX trades rose from ~18/day to 100+. reported

Case: Meghan × Outland Denim “Harriet” (October 17, 2018)

A small Australian ethical-denim brand that had sent product to her team; no paid placement. She stepped off the plane in Dubbo wearing their black Harriet jeans.

Identification
Australian press named Outland Denim the same morning (first on-topic article 11:47 a.m. Sydney time); “black Harriet” soon after. observed
Coverage
93 on-topic articles but only 12 distinct headlines: News Corp regional papers republished the same stories. observed
Search
Australian searches for “Outland Denim” went from 0 to 100 on day 0; US searches barely registered. observed
Stock
5 of 26 sizes available on day +1; 26 of 26 by day +4 with preorder switched on. observed
Sales
Traffic +3,000%, sales +640% in the week; 46 seamstresses hired. reported

Benchmarks

Kanye × Ultra Boost (May 16–17, 2015). The “triple white” shoe people remember. The adidas.com page showed a “Coming Soon” countdown on May 17–18, then out of stock by May 20, before its June 1 release. Because the release was already scheduled, this is a comparison, not a clean surprise.

Bieber × Crocs (October 2020). A preannounced collaboration that reportedly sold out in about 90 minutes. “Justin Bieber Crocs” rose 11 SD, but generic “Crocs” search did not move. This is our upper bound for an explicit, promoted deal, not an implicit ad.

Spillover: did attention spread to siblings or competitors?

For each case we chose a set of same-brand siblings (other models or colorways from the same brand) and competitors (similar products from rival brands), and compared them with the focal item on three channels: search, stock, and news coverage.

Search

  • The spike is in the pairing. “Taylor Swift New Balance” (11.6) and “Justin Bieber Crocs” (11.2) dwarf every product or brand term.
  • Generic product search moved little. “New Balance 550” 1.1; “Crocs” 0.1. Kanye’s “Energy Boost” is the exception at 2.6 (3.8 over the full week).
  • No competitor product search cleared 2 SD in any case: Dunk Low, Samba, Air Force 1, Nike Free, Flyknit, rival denim.
  • Same-brand spillover went to the nearest sibling. NB 327 reached 2.2 for Taylor. For Kanye, Ultra Boost reached 3.1 over the week, but Kanye wore Ultra Boosts himself on day +7 and the shoe had a launch countdown, so we cannot attribute it.

Search over time: every term against its own normal

Each row is one search term; each column is one day. The color shows how far that day is from the term’s own normal, in standard deviations, after adjusting for day of week. White means no change, red means above normal, blue means below normal (a possible sign of substitution). Because every row is measured against itself, rows with very different search volumes can be compared directly.

Stock

  • Kanye: only the worn colorway (B44283) went out of stock. Four sibling colorways were in stock on days +6 to +17. This is the cleanest observed evidence that demand stayed on the exact product.
  • Taylor: sibling 550 colorways mostly showed full size runs afterwards. Two depleted pages are hard to read: one was already half empty before the game, the other has no pre-event capture.

News coverage

We counted other products named near the celebrity or focal-product mention in on-topic articles (one copy per headline). Competitors rarely appear. For Meghan, the products sharing her coverage were the rest of her outfit, not other jeans: the Serena Williams jacket appears in 9 of 12 distinct stories. For Kanye, most Nike mentions are about his departure from Nike, not product comparisons. Hypebeast did ask whether “associated colorways of the same model” would get the same reception — the specialist press itself raising the sibling question.

The “Taylor Swift Nike” bar is probably noise

What the daily data show “Taylor Swift Nike” is so rare that Google reports mostly zeros with random one-day blips (79% of pre-period days are zero). Its run of non-zero days began three days before the game (28, 34, 42, 33), peaked once on day +1 (58), and was back to zero by day +4. So it is not spillover from the New Balance appearance; the 2.7 comes from comparing a few blips to a mostly-zero baseline. The chart now hatches it as sparse.

She did wear Nike four weeks later: custom red-and-white Air Force 1s at the October 22 Chiefs–Chargers game (Page Six, Highsnobiety). That is where the series shows its only sustained run (October 21–26). Highsnobiety noted Nike featured the colorway on its store, but buzz was much smaller than for the 550s, perhaps because the Air Force 1 is a staple. That makes a useful contrast case (see Next steps).

Can we trust the article coding?

We hand-labeled 65 articles for one question: is this article about this celebrity wearing this product at this appearance? The first 33 exposed errors and were used to fix the rules; the other 32 were drawn afterwards and are the honest test.

Old rule: celebrity and brand anywhere on the page0.751.000.81

Precision: of articles the coder counted, the share that were truly about the appearance. Recall: of truly relevant articles, the share it caught.

  • Errors we fixed: “Kanye” and “adidas” appear together on nearly every sneaker-site page; “Outland” matched Outlander TV recaps; related-story sidebars produced false hits; syndicated copies inflated counts.
  • Local Qwen never included a wrong article but missed about one in four, so it works as a strict filter and product extractor, not as the main coder. It labeled all 243 articles in about 8 minutes at no API cost.
  • Caveat: the gold labels were coded by the assistant from excerpts; a human should spot-check them.

What we cannot say yet

  • Nothing causal. Five hand-picked, famous events; no matched non-events; no counterfactual.
  • No unit sales. Out-of-stock pages show inventory ran short, not how much sold. Preorder can make a sold-out item look available (Outland).
  • Archived pages are patchy. No pre-event capture for Taylor’s or Meghan’s focal product; captures are irregular.
  • Search data are coarse. Google Trends is sampled, rounded, scaled within each request, and noisy for rare terms.
  • Comparison sets were chosen after a first look. They are illustrative; the real study must fix them in advance.
  • Selection. These cases are famous because they had big responses. The research question is the whole distribution, including the many appearances with no response.

Next steps

Ordered by what unlocks the most. Rough costs assume our current setup (public data, cached pipeline, local Qwen).

This week: tighten the demo

  1. Human spot-check of the 65 labeled articles. Every coverage number rests on them. A second coder also gives us inter-rater agreement. ~30–45 minutes of your or an RA’s time; I can prepare a one-page review sheet.
  2. Add Taylor × Nike Air Force 1 (October 22, 2023) as a contrast case. Same celebrity, same setting, same team colors, but a ubiquitous staple shoe from a competitor. If the response is much smaller, that is direct evidence that product distinctiveness matters. About an hour of pipeline time, no new tools.
  3. Write a pre-registration file for comparison sets. Fix siblings, competitors, windows and the primary outcome before adding any new case, so the spillover tests cannot be tuned after the fact. One short config + doc.

Next two weeks: move from anecdotes to a distribution

  1. Build the 30-appearance sample from a frozen source roster, zeros included. Pick identification sources (for example Sneaker News “celebrity feet”, outfit-ID accounts, What Pros Wear) and take every footwear appearance they identify in a fixed window, whether or not anything happened afterwards. This is what turns “famous sellouts” into the distribution of responses. Mostly curation; the pipeline already handles each case.
  2. Start prospective monitoring for new appearances. Wayback gaps are our biggest data problem. For future events we can capture the focal, sibling and competitor product pages ourselves every few hours for two weeks, plus daily search pulls. That gives clean before/after stock and size data. A scheduled job on this machine; light, polite request rates.
  3. Make search data more reliable. Pull each Trends request several times and average to reduce sampling noise; add a common anchor term so different requests can be compared; flag sparse series automatically. Small code change; more Trends requests (rate-limited).
  4. Time identification precisely. The theory says fast, exact identification concentrates demand. We have publication timestamps for articles but not for Instagram/X outfit-ID posts, which are often first. A small hand-curated log of first posts per event would fill this. ~10 minutes per event by hand.

Decisions for us

1. Do we pursue a data partner for sales?

Public data will never give units sold. A brand (New Balance, Outland), a retailer, or a resale platform could provide daily units or clicks by product for a few events. That is the only route to market outcomes beyond stockouts.

2. Retrospective, prospective, or both?

Retrospective cases are available now but have patchy archives. Prospective monitoring gives clean data but depends on which appearances happen. Recommendation: both, with the 30-appearance retrospective sample for the distribution and prospective monitoring for clean stock data.

3. What is the primary outcome?

Proposed order: (a) focal versus sibling stock depletion, which is closest to demand and directly tests concentration; (b) abnormal search for the focal product versus siblings and competitors; (c) identification speed and precision in coverage as the mechanism. Search for the celebrity–brand pairing is a manipulation check, not an outcome.
