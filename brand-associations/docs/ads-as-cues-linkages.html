Implicit Ads · Theory linkage memo

Celebrity appearances as memory cues: linking Implicit Ads to “Ads as Cues”

Bordalo, Burro, Gennaioli, Nacamulli & Shleifer (2025), NBER WP 34387 · memo of November 9, 2026 · page numbers refer to the paper’s printed pages

“Ads as Cues” models demand as depending on what comes to mind: a cue retrieves past experiences of goods that are frequent in memory and similar to the cue, and those goods crowd out others. A celebrity wearing a product is a cue of exactly this kind, and one the authors themselves gesture at: “We spontaneously look at a billboard on the street or at a brand’s name on an athlete’s shirt” (p. 10).

Bottom line

  1. Every core object in the model has a public-data analog in our setting: the cue (the appearance and its amplification), the memory database (prior familiarity with the brand or model), similarity (how close the cue is to focal, sibling and rival products), and demand (search, stockouts, resale volume).
  2. Eight testable linkages follow, from the direct effect of the cue to spillovers on siblings and rivals, spillovers to the rest of the outfit, and whether the appearance creates a lasting celebrity–product memory that later cues retrieve.
  3. The sharpest public-data test is a regional version of the paper’s exposure × habit design: Google Trends by US metro area gives exposure to the celebrity event, prior familiarity with the focal and rival brands, and the response, all from public data.
  4. Search is the wrong outcome for some predictions. The paper’s spillovers work because a cue reminds people of a product they already know; they rarely need to search for it. Those predictions need purchase-type outcomes. Archived StockX pages carry a “sales in the last 72 hours” count, which we verified for the NB 550.
  5. Our setting extends the paper in three ways: unscheduled cues (better identification than TV ad schedules), unfamiliar products (where the paper points to simulation rather than recall), and directional celebrity → product associations (the paper’s similarity is symmetric).

The model in one picture

A consumer buys a good only if its past pleasure comes to mind. What comes to mind depends on three things the paper measures with Nielsen data, and that we would measure with public data.

Cue cAn ad, a craving, a context.Ours: the celebrity appearance and its coverage.
→
Similarity S(c, x)Feature overlap between the cue and each good.Ours: how close the worn item is to the focal SKU, siblings, rivals.
×
Database f(x)How often the consumer experienced each good.Ours: prior familiarity with each brand or model.
→
Recall → demandRecall share raises predicted utility; demand follows.Ours: search, stockouts, resale volume.
rx(c) = S(c,x)·fx ⁄ Σx′ S(c,x′)·fx′ Equation (1), p. 9. The numerator is association and frequency; the denominator is interference: other similar or frequent experiences compete for recall, including a large mass of “neutral” non-branded experiences, the “fog” ads must cut through (p. 7).

Its three propositions are the backbone of the linkages below:

2. Spillovers within a category (pp. 13–14)A Pepsi ad raises Coke demand among habitual Coke drinkers; ads expand the category.A celebrity in NB 550s may remind Dunk owners of their Dunks; the sneaker category may expand.

Why celebrity cues are a useful test of the theory

  • Timing is closer to random. TV ad schedules are chosen by firms, so the paper needs the Shapiro et al. (2021) strategy and many controls to deal with targeting (pp. 29–32). An unscheduled appearance (Taylor at a Chiefs game, Kanye courtside) is not timed to demand shocks, which makes a daily event study credible.
  • One cue, sharp similarity contrasts. A single appearance has measurable similarity to the exact SKU, its sibling colorways, and rival models, so we can see whether the response follows similarity, the paper’s central new prediction.
  • We can observe identification. Coverage tells us how precisely and how fast the cue was linked to a specific product. In model terms, exact identification concentrates similarity on the focal SKU.
  • It reaches products the paper excludes. The paper studies the top two brands of 20 undifferentiated packaged-goods categories (p. 16). Fashion and sneakers are differentiated, often unfamiliar, and sold through resale markets, which tests where the theory travels.

Eight linkages, each with its exact contrast

Each linkage states the paper’s prediction, our translation, the comparison that tests it, the public measures, and what the demo already shows.

Linkage 1 · Proposition 1(b)

The appearance is a cue that raises demand for the focal product

Paper

More exposure to a brand’s ads raises its demand, even though ads carry no information (p. 12).

Ours

The appearance raises attention to and demand for the worn product, even with no paid placement.

TreatedFocal SKU, days 0–7 after the appearance
vs
ComparisonSame SKU, weekday-matched pre-period (days −28 to −1)
Prediction: positive abnormal search, size depletion, and resale volume for the focal SKU
  • Google Trends (daily); brand product pages (stock, sizes); archived StockX pages (72-hour sales, last sale).
Demo so farPresent in all cases. Cleanest: Kanye’s Energy Boost ESM out of stock on adidas.com days +6 to +10 observed; NB 550 StockX 72-hour sales 136 on day +2 observed.
Linkage 2 · Proposition 1(c)

The cue works through familiarity, and concavely

Paper

Ads work better on frequent buyers, but the effect flattens for the most frequent, who “have the good already top of mind” (pp. 12–13). Coke: 166 seconds of ads for one extra can at average frequency, 44 seconds at the 95th percentile (p. 26).

Ours

Two versions. Within an event: regions more familiar with the brand respond more to the same appearance. Across events: an inverted U. Unknown brands have no memories to retrieve; staples (Air Force 1) are already top of mind; moderately familiar products gain most.

TreatedMetro areas with high exposure to the appearance and high pre-period familiarity with the brand
vs
ComparisonMetro areas with the same exposure but low familiarity (and vice versa)
Prediction: exposure × familiarity > 0, flattening at the top of the familiarity distribution
  • Exposure: abnormal search for the celebrity by metro area in the event window (Trends “interest by region”, DMA level).
  • Familiarity: each metro’s pre-period search for the brand or model, in the same request.
  • Response: post-minus-pre change in focal search by metro area.
CaveatThe paper’s concavity is about individual purchase frequency; ours is about regional or product-level familiarity. That is an analogy to state plainly, not a direct replication. For unfamiliar brands (Outland, starting from zero searches) the paper points to simulation of future experiences rather than recall (footnote 2, p. 7), see the extensions section.
Linkage 3 · Proposition 2(d, e)

Spillovers through re-representation: the cue reminds rival owners of their own shoe

Paper

“A Pepsi ad reminds a Pepsi drinker of Pepsi, but it reminds a Coke drinker of Coke” (p. 3). Rival ads raise demand for a brand among its habitual buyers; 80 seconds of Pepsi ads buy an extra Coke for a frequent Coke drinker (p. 5).

Ours

Taylor in white/red 550s may remind habitual Dunk or Air Force 1 buyers of their own white leather sneakers. Spillover should be larger where the rival is familiar, and smaller when the cue is exactly identified or visually distinctive, because exact identification concentrates similarity on the focal SKU.

TreatedRival model demand in metros where the rival is habitual, after the appearance
vs
ComparisonSame rival in metros where it is not habitual; and generically identified vs exactly identified appearances
Prediction: positive rival spillover, rising with rival familiarity and cue–rival similarity, falling with identification precision
  • Rival purchases are the right outcome, not rival search: people do not need to search for a shoe they already know. Use archived StockX pages for rival models (72-hour sales), marketplace listings, and metro-level search only as a weaker proxy.
Demo so farNo rival search cleared 2 SD in any case. That is consistent with concentrated identification, but it is also what we would see if spillover runs through purchases rather than search, so the null is not yet informative.
Linkage 4 · Proposition 2(f)

Category expansion: the cue makes the whole category salient

Paper

“Rather than ‘stealing business’, ads for a brand make the product category more popular” (p. 14).

Ours

An appearance can raise demand for the look (“retro court sneakers”, “black skinny jeans”) as well as for the item.

TreatedCategory terms and category-level sales after the appearance
vs
ComparisonPlacebo categories of similar seasonality (e.g. running shoes, boots)
Prediction: small positive category effect, larger for familiar categories
Demo so farWeak: Australian “black jeans” +1.3 SD over days 0–3; generic “Crocs” flat during the Bieber launch.
Linkage 5 · Proposition 3 and Section 6 on complements

The outfit: similarity spreads demand to co-worn items; dissimilar wants are crowded out

Paper

Cues spill over positively to similar goods and negatively to dissimilar ones (pp. 15, 38–39). Similarity includes shared context, such as a party (p. 8); goods consumed together cue each other, like movies and popcorn (pp. 41–42).

Ours

Items worn together share the cue’s context, so the jacket and jeans in one look should both gain. Within a look, salience theory adds that attention goes to the item that contrasts most with what is expected, such as a $110 sneaker on a billionaire.

TreatedCo-worn items in the identified look
vs
ComparisonSame-category items not worn; unrelated categories
Prediction: co-worn positive; unrelated categories zero or negative; the most contrasting item gets the largest share
Demo so farMeghan: the Serena Williams jacket appears in 9 of 12 distinct stories about the jeans observed. Taylor: her New Era windbreaker reportedly sold out reported. Coverage of Taylor’s look stressed how “ordinary” the 550 was, a contrast frame we can code.
Linkage 6 · Section 5

Measure similarity, then test whether spillovers follow it

Paper

Similarity is cosine similarity between word-stem counts of short ad descriptions; competitors average 0.46, all brands 0.25 (pp. 33–35). Higher similarity makes spillovers more positive (Table 4).

Ours

Build S(cue, product) for the focal SKU, siblings and rivals, then test whether each product’s abnormal response rises with it.

  • Text: embeddings of on-topic coverage vs product names and descriptions.
  • Image: embeddings of product photos (silhouette and colorway; the white/red 550 is visually close to a white/red Dunk).
  • Population co-retrieval: Google Trends “related queries” for the celebrity–brand term, which lists what people actually searched next.
  • Attributes: brand, model, colorway, price tier, release year.
TreatedProducts highly similar to the cue
vs
ComparisonProducts of the same brand or category but low similarity
Prediction: spillover share increases with similarity, holding familiarity fixed
Linkage 7 · Section 6 and Conclusion

Encoding: the appearance stores a distinct celebrity–product memory that later cues retrieve

Paper

Branding makes near-identical goods be “stored separately” in memory (p. 9); ads also promote a brand’s “separate encoding in memory” (p. 42), and a new entrant should “differentiate anyway you can”, drawing attention to high-contrast features so it is “encoded separately from the habitual product” (p. 47).

Ours

An identified appearance tags a generic product with a celebrity (“the Taylor Swift 550”). Later cues that share context retrieve that tag, and the stored link interferes with new links for the same celebrity.

TreatedTaylor × Nike Air Force 1, Oct 22, 2023: same context (Chiefs game), staple rival shoe, after a stored Taylor–NB link
vs
ComparisonTaylor × NB 550, Sep 24, 2023: first appearance, no prior link
Prediction: smaller Nike response; Taylor–NB association re-activated by the later appearance
  • Persistence of celebrity–product searches; marketplace listing titles that add the celebrity’s name (eBay Browse API, public product nicknames); response to repeat appearances.
Demo so far (suggestive)“Taylor Swift New Balance” searches show a second cluster around day +28, when she wore Nike, not New Balance. Highsnobiety reported much less buzz for the Air Force 1 than for the 550 reported. Both search series are sparse, so treat this as a lead to test, not a finding.
Linkage 8 · Dynamics (pp. 42, 47)

Does the appearance build a database that makes later cues stronger?

Paper

Advertising’s main return may be dynamic: it keeps habitual buyers’ memories fresh and builds the database; brands that stopped advertising lost demand that was hard to recover (pp. 42, 47).

Ours

Purchases triggered by an appearance create new experiences. If so, the baseline should stay elevated, and later appearances of the same product should get larger responses.

TreatedRepeat appearances of the same celebrity–product pair; months +1 to +6
vs
ComparisonFirst appearance; pre-period baseline
Prediction: persistent baseline lift and larger responses to repeat cues for products that sold

Where our setting extends the paper

  1. Directional associations. The paper’s similarity is symmetric (p. 8). Celebrity cues are plausibly directional: Taylor → New Balance is strong after September 2023, New Balance → Taylor is weak. That matters for interference: a strong stored link from a celebrity to one brand should weaken later links from the same celebrity to rival brands (Linkage 7), the mechanism Sahni and Yang study as competitive interference.
  2. Unfamiliar products. The paper’s recall mechanism needs past experiences. For unknown brands (Outland), a cue cannot retrieve one’s own consumption; the authors point instead to simulation of future experiences from similar memories (footnote 2, p. 7; Bordalo, Burro et al. 2025). This predicts that responses to unknown products depend on similarity to familiar experiences, and on the story attached (Outland’s ethical origin), which we can code.
  3. Exogenous, observable cues. The paper infers exposure from viewership probabilities. We observe the cue’s exact timing and its identification path, so we can separate the cue (the appearance) from its amplification (coverage, shopping links), which the paper bundles into “exposure”.
  4. Differentiated goods with resale markets. Resale prices and volumes show excess demand at retail prices, which packaged goods cannot.
Limits to keep in viewThe paper’s tests use 72 million household-week observations. Ours are event studies on aggregate outcomes, so the “habit” dimension is regional or product-level, not individual. Search measures attention and consideration, not purchase. Re-representation spillovers (Linkage 3) may be invisible in search by construction.

Proposed tests, in order

  1. Metro-level exposure × familiarity for Taylor × NB 550. The closest public-data analog of the paper’s Table 2. Pull Trends interest by DMA for pre and post windows: “Taylor Swift” (exposure), “New Balance 550”/“New Balance” (focal familiarity and response), “Nike Dunk”/“Air Force 1” (rival familiarity and response). Regress the change in response on exposure, familiarity and their product. Kansas City and Chicago give natural high-exposure markets. A day of work; about 20 Trends requests; no new tools.
  2. Archived StockX panel for focal, sibling and rival models. Turns Linkage 1 and 3 into purchase-type outcomes: 72-hour sales and prices for the worn SKU, sibling colorways, and Dunk / Air Force 1 / Samba around each event. Run a terms review first. Pipeline extension using the existing Wayback code; capture coverage varies by product.
  3. Similarity measures. Text, image and related-query similarity for every product in each comparison set, so Linkage 6 can test whether spillover follows similarity. Local models (image and text embeddings); a few hours.
  4. Taylor × Nike Air Force 1 as the encoding and interference contrast. Same celebrity and context, staple rival product, after a stored link to New Balance (Linkage 7). About an hour of pipeline time.
  5. Outfit spillovers. Add co-worn items to each case’s comparison sets (Taylor’s New Era windbreaker; Meghan’s Serena Williams jacket and Maison Kitsuné shirt) and code contrast framing in coverage (Linkage 5). Config and coding changes.
  6. Cross-event familiarity curve on the 30-appearance sample. Tests the inverted U of Linkage 2 and the identification-precision prediction of Linkage 3 across events. Depends on building the sample.
